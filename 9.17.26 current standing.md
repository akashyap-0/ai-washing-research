# Current Standing — 17 September 2026

**AI, Workforce, and Corporate Framing — codebase status**

A plain-language recap of what is actually built, what has actually been run, and
what is still open. No code knowledge assumed.

---

## 1. The one-paragraph version

The project has a complete, working **data collection and text-measurement
machine**: it pulls SEC filings directly from EDGAR, cleans them, finds the AI
and workforce language inside them, and measures it. That half is done and has
been run on real data — 822 10-K documents across 25 companies, with results in
hand.

What is **not** done is the second half: the labeled training data, the custom
classifier that would replace the off-the-shelf one, and any financial outcome
data. Because of that, the measure the current results are built on is a
simpler, borrowed one, not the one the research design calls for. Everything
downstream of "does this text predict later layoffs or headcount changes" is
written but has never been run, because we have no financial panel to run it
against.

**The single biggest gap remains financial outcome data.** It is a purchasing /
access decision, not a programming task.

## 2. What exists and has been run on real data

### Filing collection (complete)

The code talks directly to SEC EDGAR's public endpoints — no vendor, no
scraping of paywalled transcripts. It resolves a ticker to a company, lists its
filings, downloads them, and pulls out named sections.

| What | Amount |
|---|---|
| Companies in the approved universe | 25 tickers, fixed in advance |
| 10-K documents collected | 822 |
| 10-Ks with a usable Item 1A (Risk Factors) | 282 |
| 8-K documents collected | 1,032 |
| Local cache of raw filing HTML | ~700 MB |

A historical backfill was run in August to reach as far back as the data allows.
Filings before roughly calendar 2006 cannot contribute at all — Item 1A Risk
Factors only became a required 10-K item for fiscal years ending on or after
1 December 2005, so for earlier years there is simply no section to extract. The
code draws that line explicitly rather than failing silently.

Two non-SEC helpers exist and are deliberately limited in scope: a reader for
Challenger, Gray & Christmas's public layoff reports, and an optional search
lookup that stores only URLs and short snippets, never full article text, for
copyright reasons.

### Text measurement (run; but see the caveat in §4)

Three things are producing real numbers today:

1. **Within-document risk severity.** For each 10-K, the code splits Item 1A
   into AI-related sentences and everything else, scores both with FinBERT, and
   reports the difference. Positive means the company frames its AI risk *less*
   severely than its other risks. 115 filings have enough AI content to score;
   77 survive a stricter "at least 5 AI and 5 non-AI sentences" filter.
2. **8-K vs. 10-K AI tone gap.** The same tone measure applied to AI sentences
   in earnings 8-Ks minus the matched 10-K.
3. **Risk factor composition panel.** A genuine firm-year panel, 282 rows, of
   structural measures rather than tone: what share of Item 1A is about AI,
   where in the risk factor ordering AI appears, how numerically specific the
   language is, and how much of it is recycled verbatim from the prior year.
   These are deliberately kept as separate measures, not folded into one index.

### Statistical testing (run, at the appropriate level)

After the methodological feedback, the tests were rebuilt to respect the fact
that repeated filings from the same firm are not independent observations —
within-firm correlation is high (lag-1 autocorrelation +0.66, ICC 0.48), so
treating 63 filings from 10 firms as 63 data points overstates significance
badly.

What now runs: exact firm-level permutation tests (each firm collapsed to one
value before group labels are reshuffled), a thin-evidence sensitivity check,
and leave-one-firm-out checks. The leave-one-out checks have been relabeled as
*sensitivity to individual firm removal* rather than "robustness," since every
fold re-uses 80–100% of the same data and is not independent evidence.

Results as they stand:

| Comparison | Firm-level result |
|---|---|
| AI-core vs. AI-peripheral | Difference −0.306, exact permutation **p = 0.024** |
| AI Infrastructure vs. AI Power Adopters | **Null** (p = 0.86) |
| Hyperscaler vs. Semiconductor (post-hoc) | Directional, not significant (p = 0.11) |
| Pooled within-document severity vs. zero | **Significant, and strengthens** under every restriction |

### Passage dataset (built in August — this is newer than the last status memo)

The corpus that a purpose-built classifier would train on now exists. From the
822 10-Ks and 1,032 8-Ks, the code matched each 8-K to the 10-K reporting window
covering it, kept only companies appearing in both forms, and split the text
into section-bounded passages of 20–220 words with stable IDs and full
provenance.

| | |
|---|---|
| Candidate passages | **8,990** |
| Companies | 23 (in both forms) |
| Matched reporting periods | **128** |
| Split by form | 5,267 from 10-K, 3,723 from 8-K |
| Annotation sample drawn | 1,200 passages |

591 passages were excluded on quality grounds and written to separate files so
the exclusions are auditable rather than invisible — 528 for being mostly
numbers (financial tables masquerading as prose, overwhelmingly from 8-K
prepared remarks) and 119 for being a Microsoft product-segment name rather than
AI content.

### Annotation (first real pass completed 1 September)

A stratified 255-passage sample was labeled under a genuinely blinded protocol:
two independent coders saw the bare passage text only — no company, ticker,
form, section, or date — and a third blinded adjudicator re-coded every field
wherever they disagreed.

| | |
|---|---|
| Passages completed | **241 of 255** |
| Both coders identical on all 13 fields | 207 / 241 (**85.9%**) |
| Adjudicated | 34 |
| Companies covered | 23 |

Prevalence: ai_risk 28.2%, ai_opportunity 19.1%, ai_adoption 17.8%,
ai_efficiency 4.6%, worker_augmentation 4.1%, generic_ai_marketing 3.3%,
ai_workforce_reduction 2.1%, explicit_ai_job_link 1.2%, neutral 54.8%.

**These are machine labels, not human ones**, and the files record that fact in
every row so no downstream code can mistake them for human-validated data. 14
passages remain open (11 need adjudication, 3 need coding from scratch); the
exact state needed to finish them is saved.

## 3. What is written but has never been run

This code is implemented, tested, and waiting on inputs. It is not stubbed or
faked — the unit test suite (12 tests) passes against all of it — but it has
never touched real data because the data it needs does not exist yet.

| Component | Waiting on |
|---|---|
| Custom multi-label classifier (training, threshold selection, held-out evaluation) | Adjudicated human labels |
| Passage scoring with that classifier | A trained model |
| Company-period aggregation and the four separate framing gaps | Scored passages |
| Financial panel merge | **Financial outcome data** |
| The lagged panel regressions with fixed effects and clustered errors | The merged panel |

The classifier code deliberately **refuses** to train on labels that are not
marked adjudicated, and the merge code deliberately **refuses** to guess a
fiscal year from a filing year. Those refusals are intentional guardrails.

## 4. The caveats that matter most

These are the things worth knowing before any number from this project is
quoted.

**a) The reported measure is not the designed measure.** The design calls for a
purpose-built classifier that separates AI *opportunity*, *risk*, *efficiency*,
and *workforce reduction* as four distinct signals. What is running today is
off-the-shelf FinBERT **sentiment polarity** — a general positive/negative tone
score — collapsed into a single number. The design explicitly warns against
collapsing the four signals into one index, and the current measure is exactly
that collapse. It should be described as such.

**b) The tone measure has a documented validity limit.** A construct-validity
check against the labeled data found that FinBERT polarity **cannot distinguish
a genuine, specific automation claim from a vague one** (d = −0.022, p = 0.77).
It separates promotional language from hedged language, which is a related but
different thing. This limitation applies to every tone number currently
reported.

**c) The results packet is out of date.** `RESULTS_PACKET.md` is thorough but its
filing counts and p-values were computed *before* two changes: a fix that
excludes false keyword matches (text where the only hit was "automatic renewal"
or similar, with no real AI term), and the historical 10-K backfill. Every
number in it needs re-verifying against the current data files before it is
quoted. This was deliberately left as a human reporting decision rather than
automated away.

**d) Thin evidence changes one headline.** Restricting to filings with at least
5 AI and 5 non-AI sentences: the pooled within-document finding gets
*stronger* (0.106 → 0.132), but the AI-core/peripheral headline loses firm-level
significance (p 0.024 → 0.127) while keeping its direction and a large effect
size. Notably, **Oracle's mean flips sign** (−0.156 → +0.062) — its "existential
framing" appears only in filings with a single AI sentence. The verbatim Oracle
disclosure quoted in the results packet stands on its own; the claim that Oracle
*systematically* frames AI risk as existential does not.

**e) Section boundaries are heuristic.** SEC HTML varies enormously by filer and
by year, so pulling "Item 1A" out of a filing is pattern-matching, not parsing a
standard format. Known mis-parses are excluded and reported separately from
substantive exclusions. Extraction quality still deserves a manual audit on a
stratified sample.

**f) Disclosure is not behavior.** The whole design measures what firms *say*.
Linking that to what they *do* is precisely what the missing financial panel is
for. Related: revenue per employee is affected by pricing, acquisitions,
divestitures, outsourcing and product mix — it is not direct physical
productivity, and employee-count definitions are inconsistent across issuers.

**g) Two intended companies are absent.** TSMC and SAP file 20-F, not 10-K, so
they have no Item 1A to extract. Apple is in the universe but has no usable AI
risk-factor data and is excluded rather than zero-filled.

## 6. What comes next, in priority order

1. **Acquire financial outcome data.** Employees, employee growth, revenue per
   employee, restructuring charges, layoffs. Nothing in steps 14–19 of the
   design can move without it, and no amount of further coding substitutes.
   EDGAR gives revenue and assets but not headcount series or market
   capitalization.
2. **Finish the human gold annotation set.** The blinded LLM pass gives a strong
   starting point and a working protocol, but the design requires two human
   coders with adjudication before the classifier may be trained. Stratifying
   the human sample by form (8-K vs. 10-K) would let us compare machine–human
   agreement across the two, which is a useful check in its own right.
3. **Train the multi-label classifier** on those labels. The code, the
   company-level train/validation/test split, and the evaluation reporting are
   already written and tested.
4. **Reconcile the results packet** against the current data files, and settle
   the eight open reporting decisions it lists — most consequentially, how to
   report the thin-evidence sensitivity and what to say about Oracle.

The honest summary: the measurement infrastructure is real and working, the
statistical discipline is in place, and the remaining work is gated on two
inputs — labeled data and financial data — rather than on more code.
